FULLMETAL ALCHEMIST

SERIES REVIEW · SPOILER-LIGHT · BOTH ANIME ADAPTATIONS
QUICK TAKE

The Elric brothers’ search for the Philosopher’s Stone gives Fullmetal Alchemist one of anime’s strongest foundations: grief, ambition, and a rule-bound magic system that keeps asking what a life is worth. The 2003 anime and Brotherhood are distinct adaptations, not interchangeable cuts. This review treats both as separate viewing experiences.

THE ELRIC BROTHERS’ JOURNEY

Crunchyroll’s official Brotherhood synopsis describes the cost of Edward and Alphonse’s attempt to break alchemy’s laws: Ed loses limbs, and Al’s soul is left attached to armor. Their search for the Philosopher’s Stone is an adventure, but the brothers’ bond and the consequences of their choice keep it grounded.

The series builds a satisfying world around that premise. Military ambition, alchemical rules, political secrets, and personal grief intersect without losing sight of the two brothers at its center. “Equivalent exchange” becomes more than a rule for magic; it gives the story a language for sacrifice, responsibility, and limits.

2003 AND BROTHERHOOD ARE DIFFERENT EXPERIENCES

The 2003 series begins from the same source material but develops its own later direction. Brotherhood follows the completed manga storyline more closely and gives the story a broad, tightly connected endpoint. The first version takes its time with the early material and offers a distinct, more melancholic interpretation.

That difference is why neither adaptation needs to erase the other. Start with Brotherhood for the most direct manga-based story. Try the 2003 series if you want an alternate take and are open to a separate conclusion. Avoid switching between them mid-story as if they were one continuous cut.

WHY IT LASTS

The series’ biggest strength is its balance: adventure and humor make room for political tension, ethical questions, and genuine emotional consequences. The supporting cast has their own convictions, while the brothers’ shared goal gives the story a clear through-line.

The tone can shift sharply, and some humor or exposition may feel broad beside the heavier material. Even so, the world’s rules and the relationship at its center give the emotional turns a lasting foundation.

VERDICT

Fullmetal Alchemist remains a rich fantasy adventure built on a powerful brotherly bond and a world where every shortcut has a cost. Brotherhood is the clearest starting point for a manga-focused adaptation; the 2003 series is a worthwhile alternate interpretation, not a replacement. Both reward viewers who like character-driven fantasy with moral weight.
