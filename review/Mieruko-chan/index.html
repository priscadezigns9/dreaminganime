MIERUKO-CHAN / SEASON 1 REVIEW

Mieruko-chan

Miko sees grotesque spirits and survives by refusing to react. The anime’s best tension comes from the cost of that performance; its repeated set-ups can also wear thin.

← BACK TO REVIEWS
THE THING SHE SEES
This review covers the 2021 television anime, Season 1 only. It is a source-informed editorial review, not a firsthand watch report.
QUICK TAKE

Mieruko-chan turns an everyday decision—pretend you cannot see the monster—into a sustained character test. Its horror and comedy work best when Miko’s composure says more than dialogue could; the season is less satisfying when it leans on another similar encounter without advancing its wider mysteries.

Dreaming Anime is a Trinidad-rooted, Caribbean anime publication for fans worldwide.

THE PERFORMANCE OF NOT SEEING

Miko Yotsuya is not trying to defeat the spirits around her. She is trying to get through the day without letting them know she can see them. That narrow objective gives the series its strongest visual joke and its most reliable source of suspense: a hideous apparition may be inches away, while Miko has to keep her face and voice ordinary.

The choice to make her response restraint rather than bravado keeps the comedy rooted in character. A tiny change in expression can carry the whole scene. The larger, grotesque spirit designs supply the shock, but Miko’s refusal to acknowledge them supplies the tension.

WHEN HORROR AND COMEDY SHARE THE FRAME

The show understands that a funny scene does not cancel a frightening image. Its abrupt shifts can make both land harder, especially when a friend’s ordinary concern or an awkward school moment collides with something Miko alone can see. Hana’s warmth gives the story a human counterweight; Miko’s protectiveness toward people around her also makes the silence feel like a choice with consequences, not just a gag.

The limitation is repetition. Several encounters use the same basic rhythm—Miko notices, freezes inwardly, and tries not to react. The formula is the premise, but the season is most engaging when a scene changes what she understands about a person or the spirits, rather than merely escalating their appearance.

VERDICT

Mieruko-chan is a horror-comedy built around an unusually readable acting problem: how do you hide fear when reacting could make things worse? Its visual contrast and Miko’s careful composure give the premise staying power. Viewers looking for a tightly resolved supernatural mystery may find the season more episodic and its central questions deliberately open.
